\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Frame-Based Eye Tracking}

Frame-based eye tracking has long been the dominant approach for gaze estimation in head-mounted and near-eye systems~\cite{Etracker2018,HETracker2025,FlatTrack2024}. These methods typically rely on RGB or infrared images to infer the pupil center, iris geometry, eyelid configuration, or gaze direction from semantically rich appearance cues. Their main advantage lies in the availability of stable spatial information, which makes relocalization, geometric fitting, and calibration-aware regression relatively straightforward. In practical systems, this often translates into reliable global recovery and strong robustness under moderate pose or appearance variation.

However, frame-based pipelines remain limited for latency-critical XR scenarios. Because they depend on dense image acquisition and repeated full-frame processing, they incur substantial bandwidth demand, memory traffic, and compute overhead. Their effective temporal responsiveness is also constrained when rapid eye motion must be tracked continuously, especially under saccades or abrupt fixation transitions. As a result, although frame-based methods provide strong semantic stability, they are not ideal as standalone solutions for low-latency on-device XR eye tracking.

\subsection{Event-Based Eye Tracking}

Event-based eye tracking has emerged as an attractive alternative for high-speed and low-power sensing~\cite{EGaze2022,ETrack2021,FACET2024,SwiftEye2024,SEEN2025}. By asynchronously recording brightness changes, event cameras naturally suppress redundant observations and provide much higher temporal resolution than conventional frame sensors. Representative methods such as E-Track directly estimate pupil states from event representations~\cite{ETrack2021}, while FACET demonstrates that geometry-aware event modeling can yield efficient and accurate pupil localization under a dense supervision setting~\cite{FACET2024}. Other works, including Swift-Eye and spiking-based pipelines, further improve robustness or efficiency through anti-blink design, adaptive event processing, and sparse computation~\cite{SwiftEye2024,EventSpiking2025}.

Despite these advantages, event-only pipelines still face a structural limitation: they are highly effective for short-horizon updates but weaker in semantic stabilization and re-initialization. When event density becomes low, appearance context changes significantly, or drift accumulates over time, event-only tracking may lose the global anchor needed for reliable relocalization. This makes event-based tracking appealing for low-latency residual update, but less sufficient as a complete standalone solution for robust XR deployment.

\subsection{Hybrid Event-Frame Eye Tracking}

Hybrid eye tracking attempts to combine the semantic stability of frames with the temporal responsiveness of events~\cite{EXGaze2025,StateShift2025,EventSeg2025}. Among prior studies, EX-Gaze is a representative example showing that frame-based relocalization and event-based tracking can be integrated to improve both robustness and responsiveness~\cite{EXGaze2025}. Other hybrid approaches explore cross-modal fusion, temporal distillation, or event-assisted segmentation in order to preserve appearance-level recovery while still exploiting the sparse temporal characteristics of event sensing~\cite{StateShift2025,EventSeg2025}.

While this direction is promising, most existing hybrid methods still focus primarily on fusion at the model level. In many cases, supervision design, runtime mode switching, and deployment structure remain weakly coupled or are treated as secondary implementation details. For practical XR eye tracking, however, the distinction between relocalization and local update is not merely architectural; it is also operational. Search and Track exhibit different requirements in terms of input context, state dependency, robustness, and latency. This suggests that hybrid eye tracking should be treated as a system-level search-and-update problem rather than as a single undifferentiated multimodal regression task.

\subsection{Deployment-Oriented and Hardware-Aware Eye Tracking}

A separate but increasingly important research direction focuses on making eye tracking practical for embedded and XR devices through deployment-aware design and hardware acceleration~\cite{EyeCoD2024,SEE2024,JaneEye2025,SubMilliMCU2025,Retina2024}. EyeCoD demonstrates the value of optics-algorithm co-design for compact eye-tracking form factors~\cite{EyeCoD2024}, whereas SEE and JaneEye show that event-driven eye tracking can be accelerated effectively on FPGA or ASIC-style hardware~\cite{SEE2024,JaneEye2025}. These efforts confirm that low-latency eye tracking cannot be evaluated solely at the model level; the execution substrate and data movement path also matter.

In parallel, recent transformer acceleration studies have shown that deployment efficiency depends strongly on how operator dependencies, memory access patterns, and execution granularity are organized in hardware~\cite{HGPIPE2025,HPTA2023,Butterfly2022}. In particular, accelerator performance is often limited not only by arithmetic cost, but also by synchronization overhead, buffering cost, and inefficient reuse across stages. These observations are directly relevant to hybrid eye tracking, where global refresh-oriented search and reuse-oriented tracking impose different execution characteristics. However, prior deployment-oriented eye-tracking systems have largely focused either on frame-based CNN pipelines or on event-only sparse paths, leaving a gap for hybrid Search/Track systems with transformer-oriented execution structure.

\subsection{Position of HBTXR}

HBTXR is positioned at the intersection of these directions. Unlike frame-only methods, it does not rely on repeated dense-image inference for steady-state tracking. Unlike event-only methods, it does not assume that local event evidence is sufficient for stable long-horizon recovery. Unlike many prior hybrid approaches, it explicitly separates Search and Track as two different operating modes with different functional and runtime roles. Finally, unlike prior deployment-oriented systems specialized to a single sensing path or a fixed inference schedule, HBTXR is designed as an algorithm-hardware co-designed hybrid tracking system in which geometric supervision, branch decomposition, runtime scheduling, and deployment mapping are considered jointly.

Accordingly, the main distinction of HBTXR is not merely that it uses both frames and events, but that it treats hybrid eye tracking as a runtime-aware and deployment-aware system problem. This perspective motivates the proposed algorithm in Section~III and the accelerator-oriented execution architecture in Section~IV.
