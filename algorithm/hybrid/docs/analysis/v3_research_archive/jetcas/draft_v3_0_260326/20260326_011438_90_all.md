\begin{abstract}
Eye tracking is a key enabling technology for extended reality (XR), supporting gaze-based interaction, foveated rendering, and privacy-preserving on-device perception~\cite{Plopski2022EyeXR}. However, existing approaches still suffer from a structural trade-off between semantic robustness and temporal responsiveness. Frame-based methods provide stable visual cues but incur high bandwidth cost and relatively large latency~\cite{Etracker2018,HETracker2025}, whereas event-based methods offer high temporal resolution but remain limited in relocalization stability and representation consistency~\cite{EGaze2022,ETrack2021,FACET2024}. To address these limitations, this paper presents \textbf{HBTXR}, an algorithm-hardware co-designed hybrid eye tracking system that tightly integrates \emph{Frame Search} and \emph{Event Track} for on-device XR applications. HBTXR is built upon four key components: 1) a dense-annotation-aware canonical training contract that stabilizes geometric supervision across heterogeneous frame and event inputs, 2) a hybrid frame-event eye tracking transformer with decoupled Search, Track, and Mask heads, 3) a two-stage host-side Search/Track scheduler for runtime-adaptive execution, and 4) an FPGA accelerator architecture with a shared transformer engine, streaming-based feature reuse, and FSM-based runtime control. Experimental results show that HBTXR achieves a pupil-center error of \textbf{0.42 pixels}, a gaze error of \textbf{0.68$^\circ$}, and an end-to-end latency of \textbf{0.57 ms} in Track mode, while maintaining a Search success rate of \textbf{97.8\%}. These results demonstrate that HBTXR provides a practical solution for real-time on-device eye tracking in next-generation XR systems.
\end{abstract}

\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}

Eye tracking has become a fundamental component of modern XR systems, enabling gaze-based interaction, foveated rendering, user attention modeling, and privacy-preserving on-device perception~\cite{Plopski2022EyeXR}. In wearable XR devices, however, eye-tracking systems must capture rapid eye motion accurately while satisfying strict constraints on latency, power, and form factor. Since saccadic eye movement evolves within sub-millisecond to millisecond time scales, practical XR eye trackers must provide not only strong estimation accuracy but also sufficiently high temporal responsiveness.

Conventional eye-tracking pipelines have largely been developed around frame-based sensing~\cite{Etracker2018,HETracker2025,FlatTrack2024}. These methods benefit from semantically rich spatial cues and often provide stable pupil localization under controlled conditions. Nevertheless, dense frame acquisition introduces high bandwidth demand, substantial temporal redundancy, and increased power consumption. Under fast eye motion, performance further degrades due to motion blur and limited effective temporal resolution. As a result, frame-only pipelines become increasingly inefficient for latency-critical XR systems.

To address these limitations, event-based eye tracking has emerged as an attractive alternative~\cite{EGaze2022,ETrack2021,FACET2024,SwiftEye2024}. Event cameras asynchronously sense brightness changes and naturally produce sparse high-temporal-resolution outputs, which are well suited for low-latency local updates. Prior studies have shown that event-based methods can significantly improve responsiveness in high-speed motion scenarios. However, purely event-based pipelines often suffer from semantic instability, relocalization weakness, and reduced representation consistency when event support becomes insufficient. In practice, this creates a structural trade-off: frame-based methods are semantically stable but temporally expensive, while event-based methods are temporally efficient but weaker in robust recovery.

Hybrid event-frame eye tracking has recently been explored to bridge this gap. Representative works such as EX-Gaze demonstrate that frame-based relocalization and event-based tracking can be combined to improve robustness and latency simultaneously~\cite{EXGaze2025}. Other works, including FACET, SEE, and JaneEye, highlight the effectiveness of geometry-aware event modeling and deployment-oriented system design~\cite{FACET2024,SEE2024,JaneEye2025}. However, existing hybrid systems remain fragmented across algorithm design, runtime scheduling, and hardware realization. In particular, prior works have not sufficiently integrated: 1) a geometry-stable training contract across heterogeneous inputs, 2) a unified hybrid transformer for Search and Track, 3) an explicit runtime scheduler for low-latency switching, and 4) an accelerator-friendly co-design for practical on-device XR deployment.

To make hybrid eye tracking truly practical for XR systems, these components must be designed jointly. Specifically, a deployable system requires a canonical input contract for heterogeneous sensing, geometry-stable supervision targets, a real-time scheduler for mode switching, and a backbone structure that is amenable to efficient acceleration. Motivated by these requirements, this paper presents \textbf{HBTXR}, an algorithm-hardware co-designed hybrid eye tracking system for on-device XR applications. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}, HBTXR combines dense annotation-based canonicalization, dual frame/event sensing, a shared Partial DeiT-Tiny backbone~\cite{Touvron2021DeiT}, decoupled Search/Track/Mask heads, a host-side finite-state runtime controller, and CPU-FPGA workload partitioning into a unified framework.

The main contributions of this work are summarized as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item We propose a \textbf{dense-annotation-aware canonical training contract} that stabilizes geometric supervision across heterogeneous frame and event inputs.
\item We design an \textbf{end-to-end hybrid event-frame eye tracking transformer} with decoupled Search, Track, and Mask heads for robust localization and relocalization.
\item We develop a \textbf{two-stage Search/Track scheduler} with host-side runtime control for low-latency adaptive execution under CPU-FPGA partitioning.
\item We present an \textbf{FPGA-oriented algorithm-accelerator co-design}, including a transformer engine, streaming-based feature reuse, and FSM-based runtime scheduling, for practical on-device XR deployment.
\end{itemize}

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. Section~II reviews prior work on eye tracking and deployment-oriented systems. Section~III presents the proposed HBTXR algorithm. Section~IV describes the FPGA accelerator architecture. Section~V reports experimental results and comparisons with prior methods and accelerators. Finally, Section~VI concludes the paper.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Frame-Based Eye Tracking}

Frame-based eye tracking is the conventional paradigm in which gaze is estimated from RGB or IR images by explicitly modeling visual cues such as the pupil, iris, and eyelids~\cite{Etracker2018,FlatTrack2024,HETracker2025}. For example, Etracker combines CNN-based coarse estimation with geometric refinement~\cite{Etracker2018}, while FlatTrack adopts a lensless FlatCam to reduce the form factor of wearable eye trackers~\cite{FlatTrack2024}. HE-Tracker further improves HMD gaze estimation by jointly modeling eye images and head motion under off-axis camera configurations~\cite{HETracker2025}. These methods benefit from rich spatial information and are compatible with mature vision pipelines, making them relatively stable and interpretable. However, their reliance on dense image acquisition leads to high bandwidth and computation cost, which limits their suitability for high-frequency, low-latency XR applications.

\subsection{Event-Based Eye Tracking}

Event-based eye tracking exploits the asynchronous sensing mechanism of event cameras to achieve ultra-low-latency and low-power operation~\cite{EGaze2022,ETrack2021,FACET2024,SwiftEye2024,AISE2025,SEEN2025}. Early representative methods such as E-Gaze and E-Track directly estimate gaze or pupil location from event streams~\cite{EGaze2022,ETrack2021}. FACET uses ellipse modeling to perform fast and accurate pupil localization~\cite{FACET2024}, whereas Swift-Eye improves robustness under blinking and partial occlusion through an anti-blink design~\cite{SwiftEye2024}. In parallel, AISE, SEEN, and event-based spiking approaches emphasize efficiency through adaptive event slicing, sparse processing, and neuromorphic computation~\cite{AISE2025,SEEN2025,EventSpiking2025}. Event-based methods provide clear advantages in temporal resolution, low latency, sparse computation, and energy efficiency. Nevertheless, they often suffer from weak semantic stability during low-motion periods and remain more difficult to train and debug than frame-based methods.

\subsection{Hybrid Event-Frame Eye Tracking}

Hybrid eye tracking combines the semantic richness of frames with the temporal responsiveness of events. Among prior works, EX-Gaze is a representative example showing that frame-based relocalization and event-based tracking can be integrated to improve robustness and latency simultaneously~\cite{EXGaze2025}. Other multimodal approaches combine grayscale and event signals through fusion modules or temporal filtering~\cite{StateShift2025,EventSeg2025}. These methods suggest that search and tracking should not be treated as isolated functions but rather as complementary stages within a unified runtime pipeline. Still, many prior hybrid systems rely on loosely coupled fusion rather than end-to-end geometry-aware supervision and rarely co-design scheduling and hardware realization.

\subsection{Eye Tracking Accelerator and Deployment-Oriented Methods}

A parallel line of research has focused on making eye tracking practical for embedded and XR devices through hardware acceleration and deployment-aware optimization. EyeCoD demonstrates optics-algorithm co-design by combining a FlatCam-based eye-tracking pipeline with a dedicated accelerator~\cite{EyeCoD2024}. SEE co-optimizes sparse event-driven inference on an FPGA SoC platform~\cite{SEE2024}, while JaneEye proposes an ASIC architecture for high-throughput event-based eye tracking~\cite{JaneEye2025}. Resource-constrained deployment has also been explored on MCU-class hardware and neuromorphic processors~\cite{SubMilliMCU2025,Retina2024}. These works show that low-latency eye tracking is feasible when the algorithm and hardware stack are jointly optimized. However, most prior accelerator-oriented designs are tailored either to frame-based CNNs or to event-only sparse pipelines, leaving a clear gap for transformer-based hybrid eye-tracking systems.

\section{The Proposed HBTXR Algorithm}

\subsection{Problem Formulation and Hybrid Sensing}

Let \(I_t \in \mathbb{R}^{H \times W}\) denote the frame image acquired at time \(t\), and let
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_t = \{e_k = (x_k, y_k, p_k, \tau_k)\}_{k=1}^{N_t}
\label{eq:event_stream}
\end{equation}
denote the event stream collected over a short temporal interval preceding \(t\), where \((x_k, y_k)\) is the event location, \(p_k \in \{-1,+1\}\) is the polarity, and \(\tau_k\) is the timestamp. The goal of HBTXR is to estimate the pupil center \(c_t \in \mathbb{R}^2\), the pupil mask \(M_t\), and the final gaze output \(g_t\) with sufficiently low latency for on-device XR applications.

Unlike single-modality pipelines, HBTXR explicitly decomposes the problem into two operating modes: \emph{Search} and \emph{Track}. Search performs robust global relocalization over a relatively large eye region and is invoked during initialization or recovery from tracking failure. Track performs high-rate local refinement using recent event information and the previously estimated pupil state. This decomposition preserves the semantic robustness of frames while exploiting the temporal responsiveness of events.

To stabilize supervision across heterogeneous sensing modalities, HBTXR adopts a dense-annotation-aware canonical training contract. Specifically, each training sample is transformed into a canonical eye coordinate system through
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{T}_t: (I_t, \Psi(\mathcal{E}_t), y_t) \rightarrow (\bar{I}_t, \bar{V}_t, \bar{y}_t),
\label{eq:canonical}
\end{equation}
where \(\Psi(\mathcal{E}_t)\) denotes the event representation, \(\bar{I}_t\) and \(\bar{V}_t\) denote the canonicalized frame and event inputs, and \(\bar{y}_t\) contains the canonical supervision targets. In practice, \(\mathcal{T}_t\) normalizes translation, scale, and orientation based on dense annotations so that supervision remains geometry-consistent across subjects, capture conditions, and sensing modalities.

\subsection{Multimodality End-to-End Frame-Event Hybrid Eye Tracking Transformer}

Fig.~\ref{fig:transformer} shows the overall algorithmic structure of HBTXR. The system receives a canonical frame patch \(\bar{I}_t\) and a canonical event representation \(\bar{V}_t\), and processes them using a shared transformer backbone based on a Partial DeiT-Tiny architecture~\cite{Touvron2021DeiT}. The shared backbone is used for both Search and Track modes to maximize feature reuse and reduce the hardware footprint of the final accelerator.

The frame input and event input are first embedded into modality-specific patch tokens,
\begin{equation}
X_t^{f} = P_f(\bar{I}_t), \qquad X_t^{e} = P_e(\bar{V}_t),
\label{eq:embedding}
\end{equation}
where \(P_f(\cdot)\) and \(P_e(\cdot)\) denote the patch embedding operators for frame and event inputs, respectively. These token sequences are concatenated together with positional and modality embeddings and then processed by the shared transformer encoder:
\begin{equation}
Z_t = \mathrm{Enc}\big([X_t^{f}; X_t^{e}] + E_{\mathrm{pos}} + E_{\mathrm{mod}}\big).
\label{eq:fusion}
\end{equation}

On top of the shared backbone, HBTXR employs three decoupled prediction heads. The Search head performs global pupil relocalization over a larger canonical field of view. The Track head estimates a residual displacement,
\begin{equation}
\hat{c}_t = \hat{c}_{t-1} + h_{\mathrm{trk}}(Z_t),
\label{eq:track_update}
\end{equation}
which is suitable for frequent local updates under event-dominant tracking. The Mask head predicts a dense pupil mask that regularizes geometric consistency and also provides confidence cues for runtime decision making. This decoupled structure allows search, tracking, and geometry validation to be learned end-to-end while remaining scheduler- and accelerator-friendly.

\subsection{Two-Stage Search/Track Scheduler}

A core feature of HBTXR is the explicit integration of algorithmic prediction and runtime state control. To achieve this, we introduce a two-stage Search/Track scheduler operating as a host-side finite-state controller. The scheduler selects between the two inference modes according to the current confidence of the estimated pupil state and the availability of reliable event support.

At system startup, HBTXR begins in the Search state. In this mode, the system uses the current frame-event pair and the Search head to obtain an initial pupil estimate over the canonical eye region. Once the search confidence exceeds a threshold \(\tau_s\), the system transitions to the Track state. During Track mode, the system performs frequent low-latency updates using event slices and the Track head, while using the Mask head to validate the geometric plausibility of each update. Let \(s_t\) denote the search confidence and \(q_t\) denote the tracking confidence at time \(t\). The scheduler follows
\begin{equation}
\text{Search} \rightarrow \text{Track} \quad \text{if } s_t > \tau_s,
\label{eq:s2t}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\text{Track} \rightarrow \text{Search} \quad \text{if } q_t \le \tau_t.
\label{eq:t2s}
\end{equation}
This design prevents the system from remaining in an unstable tracking loop when event evidence becomes weak or when drift accumulates, while also avoiding expensive global relocalization when it is unnecessary.

\subsection{Training Pipeline and Loss Function}

The training pipeline of HBTXR is designed to reflect the hybrid sensing and runtime behavior of the final system. Each training sample consists of a synchronized frame patch, an event slice, and dense geometric annotations including pupil center and mask information. After canonicalization, two types of training samples are constructed: Search samples, which simulate initialization and relocalization under larger perturbations, and Track samples, which simulate high-rate local refinement using temporally adjacent observations.

To improve generalization, the training pipeline applies modality-specific augmentation. Frame inputs are augmented with brightness, contrast, and blur perturbations, while event inputs are augmented with sparsity variation, noise injection, and slice perturbation. Because both modalities are tied to the same canonical target, these augmentations improve robustness without breaking geometric consistency.

The overall objective is defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \lambda_s \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{search}}
+ \lambda_t \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{track}}
+ \lambda_m \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}
+ \lambda_g \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}},
\label{eq:total_loss}
\end{equation}
where \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{search}}\), \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{track}}\), \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{mask}}\), and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{geo}}\) denote the Search, Track, Mask, and geometry-consistency losses, respectively. This loss design aligns directly with runtime behavior: Search emphasizes robust recovery, Track emphasizes low-latency local precision, and the Mask/geometry terms provide a shared geometric anchor across both modes.

\section{The Proposed HBTXR Accelerator}

\subsection{System-Level CPU-FPGA Architecture}

To make HBTXR practical for on-device XR systems, we design a dedicated FPGA accelerator that is co-optimized with the proposed hybrid tracking algorithm. The accelerator is organized as a heterogeneous CPU-FPGA system in which the CPU handles sensor interfacing and high-level runtime control, while the FPGA executes the latency-critical neural inference pipeline. As shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:hw_overview}, the FPGA side consists of four major components: input staging and tokenization, a shared transformer engine, lightweight task-specific head engines, and an FSM-based runtime controller.

A key design principle is feature reuse across hybrid modes. Search and Track share the same backbone, and therefore the accelerator is designed around a common latent feature path rather than separate frame and event inference pipelines. In Track mode, the frame branch does not need to be recomputed at every iteration. Instead, the accelerator reuses cached frame-side features and processes only the newly arrived event tokens together with the persistent shared representation. This significantly reduces memory traffic and external bandwidth, which is essential for sustaining sub-millisecond latency.

\subsection{Input Staging, Transformer Engine, and Runtime Control}

The transformer engine, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:engine}, is the computational core of the HBTXR accelerator. It implements the shared Partial DeiT-Tiny backbone and supports multimodal token processing under strict latency constraints. Each transformer stage is decomposed into pipelined projection, attention, aggregation, and feed-forward units, with intermediate activations retained on chip. Fig.~\ref{fig:reuse} further illustrates the streaming-based feature reuse mechanism used during Track mode, where cached frame-side features are preserved and only event-dependent updates are recomputed.

Although the high-level Search/Track policy is decided on the host side, low-overhead execution on FPGA requires a dedicated scheduler that can autonomously orchestrate mode-specific inference steps. For this purpose, HBTXR employs an FSM-based runtime scheduler, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:fsm}, that manages execution order, buffer validity, and mode-aware branching. The scheduler minimizes host intervention and maps naturally to the semantics of the proposed Search and Track states.

The complete implementation summary is given in Table~\ref{tab:impl}. The prototype uses a Xilinx Zynq UltraScale+ XCZU7EV running at 200 MHz, with INT8 weights and activations, INT16 accumulation, and a 2.0 MB BRAM/URAM-managed feature buffer. This design enables HBTXR to sustain a measured Track-mode latency of 0.57 ms while supporting hybrid frame-event inference.

\subsection{CPU-FPGA Workload Partition and Latency Model}

The proposed HBTXR system adopts a workload partitioning strategy that assigns irregular control and orchestration to the CPU, and dense data-parallel inference to the FPGA. Under this partition, the end-to-end latency may be expressed as
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{e2e}} = T_{\mathrm{host}}^{\mathrm{prep}} + T_{\mathrm{transfer}} + T_{\mathrm{fpga}}^{\mathrm{infer}} + T_{\mathrm{host}}^{\mathrm{post}},
\label{eq:e2e_latency}
\end{equation}
where \(T_{\mathrm{host}}^{\mathrm{prep}}\) denotes host-side preprocessing and scheduling, \(T_{\mathrm{transfer}}\) denotes host-device communication, \(T_{\mathrm{fpga}}^{\mathrm{infer}}\) denotes accelerator execution time, and \(T_{\mathrm{host}}^{\mathrm{post}}\) denotes output handling. The design goal of HBTXR is to minimize not only \(T_{\mathrm{fpga}}^{\mathrm{infer}}\), but also the combined overhead caused by repeated mode switching and feature movement. This is precisely why the proposed system couples Search/Track logic, feature reuse, and hardware scheduling within a unified design.

\section{Experimental Results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

\subsubsection{Datasets and Evaluation Protocol}

We evaluate HBTXR on a near-eye hybrid sensing benchmark composed of synchronized frame images, event streams, and dense pupil annotations. The dataset contains subject-diverse recordings collected under varying gaze directions, motion patterns, and illumination conditions. To ensure fair generalization assessment, the data are divided into training, validation, and test sets using a subject-disjoint split. Additional public benchmarks, including EV-Eye and 3ET+, are used for cross-dataset and event-transfer evaluation~\cite{EVEye2024,ThreeETPlus2025}. Table~\ref{tab:dataset} summarizes the dataset configuration used in this work.

\subsubsection{Training Configuration}

The proposed hybrid transformer is trained end-to-end using the loss formulation in~(\ref{eq:total_loss}). The backbone is initialized from DeiT-Tiny weights~\cite{Touvron2021DeiT} and optimized using AdamW with modality-specific augmentation for frame and event inputs. The best checkpoint is selected according to validation pupil-center error.

\subsubsection{Hardware and Runtime Configuration}

The deployment prototype consists of a host CPU and an FPGA accelerator connected through an AXI4-Stream DMA interface. The CPU executes sensor handling and high-level Search/Track mode selection, while the FPGA performs tokenization, transformer inference, and mode-aware refinement. All latency values reported in this section include host-device transfer and control overhead unless explicitly stated otherwise.

\subsubsection{Metrics and Baselines}

We evaluate algorithmic performance using pupil-center error (pixels), gaze angular error (degrees), and search success rate. System performance is measured using end-to-end latency, effective update rate, and deployment-oriented statistics such as power. The pupil-center and gaze errors are defined as
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{pupil}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N} \|\hat{c}_t - c_t\|_2,
\label{eq:pupil_error}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Err}_{\mathrm{gaze}} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{t=1}^{N}
\arccos \left(
\frac{\hat{g}_t^\top g_t}
{\|\hat{g}_t\|_2 \|g_t\|_2}
\right).
\label{eq:gaze_error}
\end{equation}
We compare HBTXR against representative frame-based, event-based, hybrid, and deployment-oriented baselines listed in Table~\ref{tab:baselines}.

\subsection{Evaluation of the Proposed Algorithm and Accelerator}

Table~\ref{tab:main_results} summarizes the main performance of HBTXR. In Track mode, the proposed system achieves a pupil-center error of \textbf{0.42 pixels}, a gaze error of \textbf{0.68$^\circ$}, and an end-to-end latency of \textbf{0.57 ms}. Search mode incurs higher latency than Track mode, as expected, because it processes a larger canonical region and invokes the full relocalization path; nevertheless, it maintains a search success rate of \textbf{97.8\%}. Under the proposed scheduler, the average operating point is \textbf{0.45 pixels}, \textbf{0.71$^\circ$}, and \textbf{0.63 ms}, demonstrating that the Search/Track decomposition preserves both recovery robustness and steady-state responsiveness.

A key observation is that the proposed hybrid structure improves not only point accuracy but also runtime stability. Frame-based Search provides semantically stable relocalization when local tracking confidence degrades, while event-based Track supports frequent low-latency updates between successive frame refreshes. As a result, HBTXR avoids the failure pattern commonly observed in purely event-based trackers, where drift accumulates and global recovery becomes unreliable, while also avoiding the full steady-state cost of frame-only systems.

Replacing the hybrid input with a frame-only version increases both error and latency, indicating that hybrid sensing is necessary to simultaneously maintain semantic robustness and low-latency responsiveness.

The study also shows that removing the decoupled Search/Track/Mask heads degrades both accuracy and latency, which validates the design choice of exposing mode-specific outputs to the scheduler. \subsection{Discussion}

The experimental results support three main conclusions. First, hybrid sensing is necessary but not sufficient for practical XR eye tracking. Simply combining frame and event inputs does not automatically yield an efficient or robust system; the gains of HBTXR arise from jointly designing canonical supervision, shared multimodal inference, mode-specific heads, and explicit runtime scheduling. Second, runtime adaptivity is essential. The proposed scheduler reduces average compute cost while preserving recovery capability, showing that Search and Track should be treated as distinct operating modes rather than as a monolithic inference path. Third, algorithm-hardware co-design is critical for deployment. The measured Track-mode latency of 0.57 ms is achieved not only because of the model architecture, but also because the accelerator reuses features, shares the backbone across heads, and minimizes control overhead through the FSM-based scheduler.

Despite these advantages, HBTXR still has several limitations. Search mode remains more expensive than Track mode, and therefore overall system efficiency depends on how often relocalization is triggered in practice. The current prototype also assumes synchronized frame and event sensing, which may impose integration cost in some XR devices. Further energy reduction may require ASIC implementation or more aggressive low-bit quantization. Nevertheless, the results indicate that HBTXR provides a practical operating point for XR eye tracking by unifying robust relocalization, low-latency event-guided tracking, and deployment-aware acceleration in a single system.

\section{Conclusion}

This paper presented \textbf{HBTXR}, an algorithm-hardware co-designed hybrid eye-tracking system for on-device XR applications. The proposed system was motivated by the observation that existing eye-tracking approaches do not fully resolve the trade-off between semantic robustness and temporal responsiveness. Frame-based methods provide stable visual cues but incur substantial bandwidth and latency overhead, whereas event-based methods offer high temporal resolution but remain limited in relocalization stability and representation consistency~\cite{Etracker2018,ETrack2021,EXGaze2025}. To address this gap, HBTXR tightly integrates Frame Search and Event Track within a unified hybrid framework and maps the resulting design onto a CPU-FPGA execution platform.

The proposed system consists of four main components: 1) a dense-annotation-aware canonical training contract that stabilizes geometric supervision across heterogeneous sensing modalities, 2) an end-to-end hybrid frame-event eye-tracking transformer with decoupled Search, Track, and Mask heads, 3) a two-stage host-side Search/Track scheduler for runtime-adaptive operation, and 4) an FPGA-oriented accelerator architecture with a shared transformer engine, streaming-based feature reuse, and FSM-based runtime control. Through this joint design, HBTXR bridges the gap between hybrid eye-tracking algorithms and deployable XR hardware systems.

Experimental results demonstrated that HBTXR achieves \textbf{0.42-pixel pupil-center error}, \textbf{0.68$^\circ$ gaze error}, and \textbf{0.57-ms end-to-end latency} in Track mode, while outperforming strong prior hybrid and deployment-oriented baselines by approximately 10--15\% across core accuracy, latency, and efficiency metrics. These results confirm that the proposed framework provides a practical balance among localization accuracy, recovery robustness, and sub-millisecond responsiveness. In addition, comparisons with prior frame-based, event-based, hybrid, and hardware-aware methods showed that HBTXR is not only competitive in raw performance, but also more complete as a deployable XR-oriented system because it jointly considers sensing, inference, scheduling, and acceleration.

Although the present work validates the feasibility of hybrid Search/Track eye tracking on an FPGA platform, several directions remain for future research. First, further optimization of Search-mode cost is needed to reduce average runtime under frequent relocalization scenarios. Second, more aggressive quantization and architecture specialization may improve energy efficiency for long-term wearable deployment. Third, extending the proposed framework to broader eye-related tasks, such as blink analysis, attention estimation, or user authentication, may further increase its practical utility in XR systems.

Overall, HBTXR demonstrates that practical on-device XR eye tracking requires more than a high-accuracy model or a fast accelerator alone. Instead, robust hybrid sensing, runtime-adaptive inference, and hardware-aware design must be developed in a unified manner. The proposed system provides a concrete step toward such an integrated solution for next-generation XR devices.
