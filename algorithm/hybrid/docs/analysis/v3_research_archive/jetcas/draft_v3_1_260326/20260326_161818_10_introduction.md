\section{Introduction}

Eye tracking has become a key enabling component of extended reality (XR) systems, supporting a wide range of applications such as gaze-based interaction, foveated rendering, attention-aware user interfaces, and privacy-preserving on-device perception~\cite{Plopski2022EyeXR}. In practical head-mounted XR devices, however, eye tracking must satisfy a set of constraints that are difficult to achieve simultaneously. The system must be semantically robust enough to recover the pupil under appearance variation and re-initialize after tracking failure, while also being temporally responsive enough to track rapid eye motion with very low latency. In addition, because XR devices operate under strict power, bandwidth, and form-factor constraints, the tracking pipeline must remain lightweight and deployment-aware rather than relying on server-side or offline-heavy computation.

Conventional frame-based eye-tracking methods provide rich spatial cues and often achieve stable pupil and gaze estimation under controlled imaging conditions~\cite{Etracker2018,HETracker2025,FlatTrack2024}. Their main strength lies in semantic stability: frames preserve detailed appearance information that is useful for relocalization, shape recovery, and calibration-aware regression. However, dense image acquisition also incurs substantial bandwidth and memory traffic, and its effective temporal resolution is limited when rapid eye motion must be tracked continuously. In XR settings, these drawbacks translate into higher latency, higher power cost, and reduced suitability for tightly integrated on-device deployment.

Event-based eye tracking has therefore emerged as a compelling alternative. By asynchronously sensing brightness changes, event cameras naturally provide sparse high-temporal-resolution observations and are well suited for low-latency tracking~\cite{ETrack2021,FACET2024,SwiftEye2024}. Prior studies have shown that event-based methods are particularly effective for fast eye motion, low-redundancy computation, and efficient runtime behavior. Nevertheless, purely event-based pipelines remain limited in semantic stabilization and recovery. When event support becomes weak, or when drift accumulates over time, event-only trackers may struggle to maintain reliable relocalization and consistent geometric estimation. As a result, frame-only and event-only paradigms expose a fundamental trade-off: the former is semantically stable but temporally expensive, whereas the latter is temporally efficient but less reliable for global recovery.

Hybrid event-frame eye tracking has recently been explored to bridge this gap by combining stable frame-based relocalization with fast event-based updates~\cite{EXGaze2025,StateShift2025,EventSeg2025}. This direction is promising, but existing hybrid systems still exhibit two important limitations. First, many prior methods focus primarily on model-level fusion while treating supervision, runtime mode control, and deployment structure as loosely connected design choices. Second, the algorithmic structure is often not aligned with hardware-oriented execution, even though practical XR tracking ultimately depends on how the search, update, and memory paths are scheduled on-device. In other words, robust hybrid eye tracking requires not only multimodal fusion, but also a system-level co-design spanning supervision, model structure, runtime control, and accelerator mapping.

This work addresses that gap through \textbf{HBTXR}, an algorithm-hardware co-designed hybrid eye-tracking system for on-device XR applications. The core idea of HBTXR is to explicitly separate \emph{Frame Search} and \emph{Event Track} within a unified hybrid tracking framework. Search is responsible for robust relocalization and anchor refresh using semantically stable frame cues, whereas Track performs low-latency residual updates conditioned on the previous state and recent event observations. To support this separation, HBTXR introduces a dense-annotation-aware geometric supervision contract, a shared-backbone hybrid transformer with decoupled prediction heads, and a host-controlled Search/Track runtime scheduler. Furthermore, the system is designed with deployment in mind: rather than assuming a uniform execution path, HBTXR adopts a mode-aware architecture in which refresh-heavy Search and reuse-heavy Track can be mapped differently under a shared accelerator-oriented backbone.

The proposed design is motivated by three observations. First, hybrid eye tracking should be formulated as a \emph{search-and-update} problem rather than as a single monolithic prediction problem. Second, geometry-stable supervision is essential for making frame-based recovery and event-based residual tracking consistent with one another. Third, deployment efficiency depends not only on model size, but also on how the execution path is structured. In particular, Search and Track exhibit different computational characteristics: Search requires broader feature refresh and stronger global robustness, whereas Track benefits from state persistence, feature reuse, and lower-overhead execution. This asymmetry suggests that the hardware architecture should not treat all inference stages uniformly, but instead support a mode-aware execution strategy with mixed granularity across refresh and update paths.

Based on these considerations, this paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item We propose a \textbf{dense-annotation-aware hybrid supervision framework} that stabilizes geometric targets across heterogeneous frame-event inputs and supports a unified search-and-track formulation.
\item We design a \textbf{hybrid Search-Track transformer} with a shared backbone and decoupled Search, Event, Track, and Mask heads, enabling robust relocalization together with low-latency residual tracking.
\item We develop a \textbf{runtime-aware Search/Track scheduler} that uses confidence, similarity, event density, and eye-state signals to switch between refresh-oriented and update-oriented operating modes.
\item We present an \textbf{FPGA-oriented co-design architecture} that maps HBTXR onto a shared transformer execution substrate with mode-adaptive dataflow, feature reuse, and low-overhead runtime control for on-device XR deployment.
\end{itemize}

Finally, Section~VI concludes the paper.
